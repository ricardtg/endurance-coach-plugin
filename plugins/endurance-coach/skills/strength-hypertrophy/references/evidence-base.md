# Evidence Base: Hypertrophy and Strength Programming

This file documents the research behind every programming choice in `strength-hypertrophy`. Citations are author-year; full references at the bottom. The plugin is opinionated but evidence-anchored. When you change a prescription, update this file and add the reference.

## 1. Volume as the Primary Driver

Hypertrophy is volume-mediated within a productive range. Total weekly hard sets per muscle group is the cleanest dose variable.

- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger 2017** (meta-analysis): dose-response relationship between weekly sets and hypertrophy; 10+ sets per muscle per week produced significantly more growth than less than 5 sets. Effect plateaus or inverts at very high volumes.
- **Baz-Valle et al. 2022** (systematic review): the dose-response is real but individual variability is high; optimal volume varies person to person.
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2019** (volume meta): linear up to ~10 sets/muscle/week, with continued (smaller) gains up to ~20.

**Plugin policy**: prescribe 10 to 20 sets per muscle per week, biased low at start and progressed individually. Use the MEV to MRV ladder (see section 4).

## 2. Rep Range and Load

Hypertrophy is similar across rep ranges from ~5 to ~30 if proximity to failure is matched.

- **Schoenfeld et al. 2017** (load meta): no significant difference in hypertrophy between heavy load (over 60% 1RM) and light load (under 60% 1RM) when sets taken to or near failure.
- **Lopez et al. 2021** (systematic review): low-load (low RIR) and high-load training produce similar muscle growth.

**Plugin policy**: most working sets in 6 to 12 reps (logistic sweet spot: enough load for strength carryover, low enough fatigue per set to keep volume up). Include heavier (4 to 6) for compound progression carryover and lighter (12 to 20) for isolation work and joint-sparing options.

## 3. Proximity to Failure (RIR / RPE)

Productive set quality requires getting close to failure. Too far and growth signal is weak; too close (constant failure) and fatigue compromises volume.

- **Refalo, Helms et al. 2022** (RIR meta): sets stopped at RIR 0 to 3 produce equivalent hypertrophy; RIR 4+ produces less.
- **Schoenfeld and Grgic 2021** (failure review): training to failure on every set is not necessary for hypertrophy and may be counterproductive due to recovery cost.
- **Helms et al. 2017** (RIR-based RPE scale): RIR 0 = RPE 10, RIR 1 = RPE 9, RIR 2 = RPE 8, RIR 3 = RPE 7. Reliable in trained athletes.

**Plugin policy**: prescribed RPE 7 to 9 (RIR 1 to 3) on working sets. RPE 10 (failure) reserved for last set of isolation exercises or rare AMRAP tests. Heavy compounds: RIR 2 to 3 (RPE 7 to 8) to manage technique and fatigue.

## 4. MEV, MAV, MRV (Volume Landmarks)

Framework popularized by Israetel and Renaissance Periodization, adapted from periodization literature.

- **MV (Maintenance Volume)**: the minimum to preserve gains during a deload or non-emphasis phase.
- **MEV (Minimum Effective Volume)**: the lowest dose that produces growth in a hard training block. Usually 8 to 12 sets/muscle/week for trained athletes.
- **MAV (Maximum Adaptive Volume)**: the range where most growth happens, 12 to 20 sets/muscle/week.
- **MRV (Maximum Recoverable Volume)**: the ceiling above which recovery breaks down. Highly individual; signaled by stalled lifts, joint pain, sleep disruption, mood drop.

The point of the framework is **progression over the block**: start near MEV, add 1 to 2 sets per muscle per week, climb toward MRV, then deload before crashing through it.

**Plugin policy**: each hypertrophy block begins at the bottom of MAV and adds 1 to 2 sets/muscle every 1 to 2 weeks until early MRV signs, then deload.

## 5. Frequency

For equal total volume, splitting it across 2+ sessions per muscle per week is at least as effective as 1 session, and often slightly better.

- **Schoenfeld et al. 2019** (frequency meta): 2x/week beats 1x at equal volume; 3x is not consistently better than 2x.
- **Grgic et al. 2018** (review): frequency matters mainly as a volume-distribution tool.

**Plugin policy**: every muscle group hit at least 2x per week. Upper/Lower 4x and PPL 6x both meet this. Upper/Lower 3x meets it for upper (twice) but not always for lower (once); compensate with more volume per lower session.

## 6. Progressive Overload (Mechanisms and Methods)

Overload mechanisms (any of these can drive adaptation):
- More weight at the same reps and RIR.
- More reps at the same weight and RIR.
- More sets per session.
- More sessions per week (frequency).
- Better technique at the same load (range of motion, control).
- Reduced rest intervals at the same load (density, less hypertrophy-relevant).

The most evidence-based method for a strength-hypertrophy block is **double progression**: pick a rep range, start at the bottom, add reps every session until top of range achieved on all working sets, then increase weight and reps drop back.

- **Plotkin et al. 2022**: confirms double progression vs autoregulated progression produces similar results when both push proximity to failure.
- **Helms et al. 2018 (Muscle and Strength Pyramid)**: practical synthesis of progression schemes for the lifter.

**Plugin policy**:
- Default rep ranges: compounds 4 to 6 or 6 to 8, accessory 8 to 12, isolation 12 to 15.
- Increment: barbell +2.5 kg, dumbbell +2 kg (per hand), machine smallest plate.
- When stalled: microload (+1 to 1.5 kg), drop rep range and rebuild, swap variant, or deload.

## 7. Deloads

Deloads are programmed recovery weeks to dissipate fatigue accumulated during a hard block. They are not optional in serious training.

- **Pritchard, Keogh et al. 2015** (review): deload practices in strength athletes; recovery weeks improve subsequent block performance.
- **Bell et al. 2020**: planned deloads improve long-term strength outcomes vs no deload.
- **Stone, Stone, Sands 2007** (textbook foundational): periodization theory; tapering and unloading periods are required for supercompensation.

**When to deload**:
- Time-based: every 4 to 6 weeks of progressive overload.
- Auto-regulated: HRV trending down 7+ days, RHR elevated, sleep disrupted, joint pain, lifts stalled across multiple exercises, motivation crash.

**How to deload (strength)**:
- Drop volume to 40 to 50% (cut sets, not weight).
- Maintain intensity (do not lower the load substantially; the goal is recovery while preserving neural pattern).
- One light week is typically enough; 5+ days for advanced athletes with high accumulated load.

**Concurrent deload (hybrid)**: when running is active, deload both modalities the same week. Running: 30 to 40% volume reduction, maintain intensity. Recovery metrics (HRV, sleep, RHR) reflect total load.

## 8. Stretch-Mediated Hypertrophy (Range of Motion)

A growing line of research suggests training muscles in a lengthened position produces more hypertrophy than the same exercise in a shortened range.

- **Wolf, Schoenfeld et al. 2023** (systematic review): partial reps emphasizing the lengthened range produced equal or greater growth than full-range, in several studies.
- **Kassiano et al. 2023**: lengthened-bias training significantly increased muscle thickness more than shortened-bias.
- **Maeo et al. 2021**: long-length partials produced more hypertrophy than full-range in seated leg curl.

**Plugin policy**:
- Prioritize exercises that load the muscle in a stretched position when available:
  - Quad: leg press deep, Smith squat with long range, ATG (ass-to-grass) back squat.
  - Hamstring: seated leg curl over lying leg curl, RDL with controlled stretch.
  - Pec: dumbbell bench (deeper than barbell), low-incline DB, deficit push-ups.
  - Lat: pullover, lat pulldown with long stretch at top.
  - Triceps: overhead variations (cable or DB).
  - Calf: standing calf (gastrocnemius, full stretch), seated for soleus.
  - Glute: deep hip flexion variants (deficit RDL, ATG squat).
- Where the lengthened bias matters less (delts, biceps), exercise selection is more flexible.

## 9. Protein and Nutrition (Context Only)

Hypertrophy literature converges on a protein intake range that supports muscle protein synthesis.

- **Morton et al. 2018** (meta-analysis, n=49 studies): 1.6 g/kg/day is the dose where additional protein produces diminishing returns; an upper bound around 2.2 g/kg/day captures the rest of the distribution.
- **Helms et al. 2014** (natural bodybuilders review): 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg/day, distributed across 3 to 5 meals.

**Plugin policy**: state 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg/day as evidence-based context if the athlete asks about protein. Do not prescribe a specific intake, do not estimate calories, do not design diets. Refer to a registered dietitian for nutrition planning.

## 10. Realistic Mass Gain Rates

- **Helms et al. 2014, Eric Helms model** (synthesis of training-age literature):
  - Year 1 of training: up to ~1 kg/month lean mass.
  - Year 2: ~0.5 kg/month.
  - Year 3+: 0.25 kg/month or less.
- For trained hybrid athletes (concurrent running): 60 to 70% of strength-only gains in lower body (Wilson et al. 2012, see section 12).

**Plugin policy**: communicate 0.25 to 0.5 kg lean mass/month, 1 to 3 kg per 8 to 12 week block, as the realistic expectation. Anything above suggests fat gain too; anything below 0.1 kg/month sustained suggests under-eating or under-recovering.

## 11. Sleep and Recovery

Sleep is the most potent recovery variable. Chronic under-sleep impairs both hypertrophy and strength outcomes.

- **Dattilo et al. 2011**: sleep deprivation reduces protein synthesis and increases proteolysis, net catabolic.
- **Knowles, Drinkwater et al. 2018**: athletes sleeping under 6 hours show measurable strength performance decrements.
- **Walker 2017** (book): chronic sleep restriction degrades training adaptation and recovery markers.

**Plugin policy**: surface sleep data in the morning report (Garmin sleep). Flag chronic under 7 h. Do not prescribe sleep schedules, but recommend professional support if patterns persist.

## 12. Concurrent Training Interference

Running compromises lower-body hypertrophy when volume and intensity are high; upper body is unaffected.

- **Wilson et al. 2012** (meta-analysis): concurrent endurance training compromised hypertrophy by ~9% and strength by ~28% on average. Effect scales with endurance volume and intensity (especially running, less for cycling).
- **Schumann et al. 2022** (systematic review): order of training (strength before vs after endurance) does not appreciably change hypertrophy outcomes. Modality and volume of endurance matter more.
- **Hickson 1980** (classic): the "interference effect" originally documented; mechanism likely AMPK/mTOR antagonism plus residual fatigue.

**Plugin policy**:
- When running is active during a hypertrophy block, expect ~60 to 70% of strength-only lower body gains.
- Keep running volume under ~50 km/week during hypertrophy when feasible.
- No quality running within 24 h of heavy lower.
- Cycling is less interfering than running; swimming and rowing even less.

## 13. Splits Beyond Upper/Lower

Upper/Lower is the default in this plugin because it scales well from 3 to 4 sessions per week and respects the 2x/week frequency rule. Other splits with similar evidence:

- **Full Body 3x/week**: ideal for beginners or limited time. Lower per-session volume per muscle, higher frequency.
- **Push/Pull/Legs 6x/week**: high-volume option for advanced athletes with time. Allows specialization phases.
- **Body-part split (bro split)**: 1x/week per muscle. Less optimal at equal volume (Schoenfeld 2019). Not recommended.

**Plugin policy**: Upper/Lower default. If athlete has 5+ sessions/week available and is advanced, PPL is acceptable. Full Body for beginners or constrained schedules.

## 14. 1RM Estimation

- **Epley formula**: `1RM = weight × (1 + reps/30)`. Most accurate for 3 to 10 rep ranges.
- **Brzycki formula**: `1RM = weight × (36 / (37 - reps))`. Slight differences from Epley; both within 5 to 10% true 1RM (Lesuer et al. 1997).
- Above 10 reps, all formulas degrade in accuracy.

**Plugin policy**: Epley for sub-10 rep tracking. Above 10 reps, track working weight progression rather than relying on 1RM estimates.

## 15. Open Questions / Active Areas

These topics are evolving; treat current plugin defaults as best available evidence subject to revision:
- Optimal exercise tempo (controlled eccentric appears best; specific cadences less clear).
- Drop sets and supersets as intensity techniques (some evidence of equivalent hypertrophy in less time, but more fatigue cost).
- Cluster sets and rest-pause (small-sample studies, promising for advanced athletes).
- Cardio modality interference ranking (running > cycling > rowing > swimming, but with overlap).
